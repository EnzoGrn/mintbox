# Aggregates

An aggregate is a group of objects that changes together and protects its own rules. Any
change goes through the aggregate root, which refuses anything that would break an
invariant.

## Design rules

These rules apply to every aggregate:

1. **Reference other aggregates by identifier only.** A `Listing` holds a
   `CatalogEntryId`, never a `CatalogEntry` object. This keeps each aggregate small and
   loadable on its own.
2. **Change one aggregate per transaction.** Other aggregates react to domain events in
   their own transaction. There is a single, documented exception: the
   [checkout](#the-checkout-exception).
3. **No setters.** State changes through methods named after business actions
   (`publish()`, `reserve()`, `counter()`), which check the rules before changing
   anything.
4. **Typed identifiers.** Each aggregate has its own identifier type (`ListingId`,
   `OrderId`...), a record wrapping a UUID. Passing a `MemberId` where a `ListingId` is
   expected doesn't compile.
5. **Identifiers are generated by the application**, as time-ordered UUIDs (version 7).
   An aggregate has its identity before it's saved, and the time ordering keeps database
   indexes compact.
6. **Optimistic locking.** Each aggregate carries a version number. Two concurrent changes
   to the same aggregate can't both succeed: the second one fails and is retried or
   reported.
7. **The aggregate records its events.** A business method registers the domain events it
   causes, and they are published when the aggregate is saved, in the same transaction.

## Catalog entry

`CatalogEntry` is reference data with little behavior. It is an aggregate mainly because
it has its own identity and lifecycle.

| Field | Notes |
|-------|-------|
| `productLine` | Amiibo for now. See [ADR 0006](../adr/0006-multi-product-line-catalog.md). |
| `sourceReference` | The identifier in the catalog source, used to synchronize. Unique per product line. |
| `name`, `character`, `series`, `franchise`, `figureType` | Attributes shared by all product lines. |
| `releaseDates` | One optional date per region. |
| `imageUrl` | A link to the image at the source. I don't host official images. |
| `attributes` | Attributes specific to one product line, validated against that line's definition. |

- **CAT-1.** `sourceReference` is unique within a product line.
- **CAT-2.** A catalog entry is never deleted, because listings and collections reference
  it. An entry that turns out to be a duplicate is marked as merged into another one.

## Collection item and wishlist entry

I deliberately did **not** make the collection one big aggregate. A collector can own
hundreds of figures, and no rule needs to look at all of them at once: series progress is
a query, not an invariant. A large aggregate would be slow to load and would make two
unrelated changes (adding a figure, removing another one) conflict.

So each **collection item** and each **wishlist entry** is its own small aggregate.

- **COL-1.** A collection item references an existing catalog entry.
- **COL-2.** A member has at most one wishlist entry per catalog entry. This rule spans
  several aggregates, so a unique constraint in the database enforces it.
- **COL-3.** When an order is completed, the item is added to the buyer's collection. If
  the seller's listing was created from one of their collection items, that item is removed
  from their collection. To know this, a collection item remembers the listing it was put
  up for sale in.

## Listing

The root of the marketplace. It holds the price, the listed items, the photos and the
delivery methods.

- **LST-1.** A listing has exactly one listed item for now. The model already holds a list,
  so that lots can be added later without changing the other modules.
- **LST-2.** To be published, a listing needs at least one photo (at most 10), a price of
  at least 1.00 EUR, and at least one delivery method.
- **LST-3.** The price, the items and the delivery methods can only change while the
  listing is a draft, active or paused. A reserved or sold listing is frozen.
- **LST-4.** Only an active listing can be reserved.
- **LST-5.** Lowering the price of an active listing publishes `ListingPriceDropped`, so
  that members who saved it can be notified.
- **LST-6.** An active listing expires after 90 days without being sold. The seller can
  renew it.

The lifecycle is described in [state machines](state-machines.md#listing).

## Negotiation

The original plan had `Offer` as an aggregate. I changed it to **`Negotiation`**, holding
the offers as entities, because the important rules span several offers:

- only one offer can be pending at a time,
- only the party who did *not* make the pending offer can accept, decline or counter it,
- a buyer can't flood the seller with offers.

With one aggregate per offer, these rules would have to live in database constraints or in
a service, outside the domain. With a `Negotiation` aggregate, they are plain checks in one
class, covered by unit tests.

A negotiation is identified by the pair (listing, buyer): there is at most one per buyer
and per listing.

- **NEG-1.** The listing must be active and marked as negotiable, and the buyer can't be
  the seller.
- **NEG-2.** An offer must be strictly lower than the listing price, and at least 50% of
  it.
- **NEG-3.** At most one offer is pending at a time. Countering an offer marks it as
  countered and creates a new pending offer from the other party.
- **NEG-4.** Only the recipient of the pending offer can accept, decline or counter it.
  Only its author can withdraw it.
- **NEG-5.** A pending offer expires after 48 hours.
- **NEG-6.** A buyer can make at most 5 offers in a negotiation.
- **NEG-7.** Once an offer is accepted, the buyer can check out at the agreed price for
  24 hours. After that, the agreement lapses and the negotiation is open again.
- **NEG-8.** Accepting an offer doesn't reserve the listing. If another buyer pays first,
  the negotiation is closed. This matches how second-hand marketplaces usually work, and
  avoids a seller locking a listing by accepting an offer that is never paid.

## Order

The purchase of one listing by one buyer. It holds a snapshot of everything agreed at
checkout, so that later changes to the listing or the fee rules don't affect it.

| Field | Notes |
|-------|-------|
| `listingId`, `buyerId`, `sellerId` | References by identifier. |
| `itemPrice` | The listing price, or the agreed price of a negotiation. |
| `shippingCost` | Zero for a hand delivery. |
| `buyerProtectionFee` | Computed at checkout. |
| `total` | `itemPrice + shippingCost + buyerProtectionFee`. |
| `deliveryMethod`, `shippingAddress` | Copied at checkout. |

- **ORD-1.** The buyer can't be the seller.
- **ORD-2.** All amounts are in the same currency, and `total` is always the exact sum of
  its parts.
- **ORD-3.** An order waiting for payment is cancelled after 15 minutes, which releases
  the listing.
- **ORD-4.** A paid order must be shipped (or handed over) within 5 days. Otherwise it is
  cancelled and the buyer is refunded.
- **ORD-5.** After delivery, the buyer has a 2-day protection window to open a dispute.
  When it ends without a dispute, the order is completed and the seller is paid.
- **ORD-6.** A partial refund can't exceed the amount paid, and the sum of all refunds on
  an order can't exceed its total.

The lifecycle is described in [state machines](state-machines.md#order).

### The checkout exception

Checking out changes two aggregates in the same transaction: it **reserves the listing**
and **creates the order**. This breaks rule 2 on purpose.

The alternative would be to create the order, then reserve the listing asynchronously in
reaction to an `OrderPlaced` event. But then two buyers could both reach the payment page
for the same listing, and one of them would be told afterwards that it was already sold.
The buyer needs an immediate yes or no.

So `ordering` calls `listing`'s public API to reserve the listing within the checkout
transaction. If two buyers check out at the same time, optimistic locking on the listing
lets only one of them succeed, and the other one gets a clear error. A dedicated
concurrency test covers this case.

This is acceptable here because both aggregates live in the same database, in a
monolith. If `listing` ever became a separate service, this would have to become a
reservation with a compensating action.

## Business model

Mintbox is not a real business, but the money flow shapes the code, so I picked a model
close to existing second-hand marketplaces:

- **The seller pays nothing** and receives the full item price.
- **The buyer pays a buyer protection fee**: 0.70 EUR + 5% of the item price, rounded half
  up to the cent. It is the platform's only revenue for now.
- **Shipping** is paid by the buyer and goes to the carrier.
- **Paid promotion** of listings ("boost") is a possible later addition. It would be a
  separate purchase, not part of an order.

I prefer a buyer fee to a seller commission because it makes the seller's side simpler (you
get exactly the price you set), and it keeps the fee visible to the buyer at checkout, which
is where the protection it pays for applies.
